\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This internship project report focuses on the development and implementation of an intelligent retail agent named "PantryPal," designed to revolutionize the digital grocery sector by mitigating high cognitive load and operational inefficiencies. The global retail sector is currently undergoing a fundamental paradigm shift, transitioning from static, catalog-based interaction models to dynamic, autonomous, and agentic experiences driven by Artificial Intelligence. While e-commerce has matured significantly, the online grocery sector remains plagued by friction, creating a disparity between user intent and system requirements that manifests as staggering cart abandonment rates and profound food waste. PantryPal represents a theoretical yet technologically viable architecture for an Intelligent AI-Driven Grocery \& Subscription Agent designed to resolve these systemic failures. By synthesizing state-of-the-art advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs), deterministic low-latency hardware (Groq LPUs), and vector-based semantic retrieval, the system proposes a new operating system for the modern kitchen—moving beyond mere transaction facilitation to proactive household management. The project involves comprehensive data integration from the Instacart Market Basket Analysis dataset, the development of a "Zero Trust" security architecture to defend against prompt injection, and the implementation of a conversational interface that reduces the cognitive tax on consumers. Despite challenges such as data integration complexities and the need for self-healing code to ensure interface reliability, the developed agent demonstrates significant potential in improving healthcare accessibility through better nutrition, optimizing supply chain forecasting, and promoting sustainable consumption. This work contributes to the emerging field of agentic AI, providing a blueprint for the future of autonomous commerce.
\\

\textbf{\textit{Keywords:}} Agentic AI, Large Language Models, Groq LPU, Vector Search, Cognitive Load Theory, Supply Chain Optimization, Food Waste Reduction, Conversational Commerce, Prompt Injection, Zero Trust Architecture.

\section{Overview/Background of the Work}

The global retail sector is currently experiencing a seismic shift, moving away from the static, catalog-based interaction models that defined the Web 2.0 era toward dynamic, autonomous, and agentic experiences driven by Artificial Intelligence (AI). While general e-commerce has matured significantly over the past two decades, the online grocery sector remains plagued by friction, high cognitive load, and operational inefficiencies. These inefficiencies manifest most visibly as staggering cart abandonment rates and profound levels of food waste.

“PantryPal,” the subject of this comprehensive internship project, represents a theoretical yet technologically viable architecture for an Intelligent AI-Driven Grocery & Subscription Agent designed to resolve these systemic failures. By synthesizing state-of-the-art advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs), deterministic low-latency hardware, and vector-based semantic retrieval, PantryPal proposes a new operating system for the modern kitchen—one that moves beyond mere transaction facilitation to proactive household management.

Grocery shopping is distinct from other e-commerce categories due to its high frequency, high basket volume, and the complexity of its decision matrix. Unlike buying a television or a pair of shoes—discretionary purchases made infrequently—grocery shopping is a recurring, non-discretionary necessity involving the selection of 30 to 50 distinct items per session. The current digital interface for this task, the Graphical User Interface (GUI), was designed for the low-velocity retrieval of information, not the high-velocity management of household inventory. The “search-scroll-filter-click” loop required to fill a digital cart imposes a significant cognitive tax on the consumer, creating a disparity between the user’s intent (“I want to cook dinner for the week”) and the system’s requirements (“Find 20 individual ingredients, check stock, and compare prices”).

This friction is not merely an inconvenience; it is an economic hemorrhage. Industry data shows that the average online grocery cart abandonment rate is extremely high, with mobile users exhibiting even higher failure rates. This suggests that the majority of digital shopping sessions end without completion, driven by complex navigation, decision paralysis, and the sheer effort required to finalize a transaction. A large portion of users abandon carts due to unexpected costs or checkout complexity, while others explicitly cite long and complicated checkout processes as the primary reason for leaving.

Furthermore, the disconnect between purchasing and consumption contributes to a global crisis of food waste, where households discard significant portions of their purchased food due to poor planning and spoilage, while retailers struggle to accurately forecast demand for perishable goods.

The emergence of Agentic AI—systems capable of perception, reasoning, planning, and tool execution—offers a solution to these entrenched problems. Unlike traditional chatbots, which are reactive and limited to scripted responses, AI agents like PantryPal can autonomously navigate complex environments, manage state over time, and execute multi-step workflows to achieve high-level goals. Powered by Large Language Models (LLMs) that act as reasoning engines, these agents can interpret natural language with near-human nuance, allowing users to interact with retailers through the most intuitive interface available: conversation.

However, deploying such agents requires a rigorous technical architecture. It demands inference speeds that closely mirror human reaction times to preserve conversational flow—requirements that traditional GPU-based infrastructures often struggle to meet but which newer specialized hardware architectures are designed to support. It also requires robust semantic memory to understand and retain user preferences without constant repetition, enabled through vector-based databases. Finally, it necessitates a strong security posture capable of defending against emerging threats such as prompt injection, where malicious inputs could manipulate agent behavior through corrupted or adversarial data.

\section{Objectives}

The primary objective of this project is to design, develop, and simulate the architecture of PantryPal, an intelligent grocery agent that effectively automates the management of household inventory and procurement. The specific objectives include:

This involves understanding the psychological barriers to online grocery adoption and how agentic interfaces can mitigate them.

This includes selecting appropriate LLMs (e.g., Kimi k2) that balance performance with cost.

\textbf{Analyze the User Experience}:  Map the paradigm shift from "searching" for products to "managing" outcomes through features like predictive subscriptions, automated inventory tracking, and conversational discovery. The goal is to move the user from a high-friction "search-scroll-filter" loop to a low-friction "intent-action" loop.

\textbf{Evaluate Economic Implications}:  Quantify the potential impact of the PantryPal system on critical economic metrics such as cart abandonment rates, average order value (AOV), and supply chain efficiency. By reducing the friction of purchasing, the agent aims to recover a portion of the \$18 billion lost annually to spoilage and inefficiencies.

\textbf{Address Security and Governance}: Explore and mitigate the critical risks associated with deploying autonomous agents in retail, specifically focusing on the threat landscape of prompt injection and the necessity for data privacy and "Zero Trust" architectures. This involves implementing guardrails that prevent the agent from performing unauthorized actions or leaking sensitive user data.8

By accomplishing these objectives, PantryPal serves not just as a shopping tool, but as a comprehensive operating system for the modern kitchen, aligning economic savings for the consumer with operational efficiency for the retailer.

\section{Scopes}

The scope of the PantryPal project encompasses the end-to-end development lifecycle of an agentic AI system tailored for the grocery domain. Key areas of development and analysis include:


\textbf{Adapted for Retail:}
 This scope is adapted in PantryPal as \emph{Pain Point Analysis and Needs Assessment}. The system processes natural language descriptions of user needs (e.g., ``I need a high-protein dinner that is quick to cook'') and maps them to relevant product inventories and recipes. This involves transforming traditional symptom-checking logic into dietary requirement and preference analysis.

\textbf{Product Discovery :}
This component focuses on developing a sophisticated recommendation engine that matches user dietary profiles and immediate needs with appropriate products. It applies advanced filtering based on price, nutritional value, and stock availability, effectively translating user hunger or shopping intent into actionable purchase recommendations.

\textbf{Multi-Criteria Search Functionality:}
Users can search for products using multiple parameters such as dietary restrictions (e.g., Keto, Vegan), price ranges, brand preferences, and sustainability indicators. The system supports both structured queries and natural language prompts, enabling requests such as ``Find me a sustainable coffee brand under \$15''.

\textbf{Data Management and Integration:}
This scope involves building robust data pipelines for ingesting and synchronizing heterogeneous datasets, including product catalogs, user purchase histories, and nutritional databases. The system ensures data accuracy, consistency, and timely updates to support reliable recommendations and planning.

\textbf{User Experience and Interface Design:}
The project includes designing an intuitive conversational interface that guides users through meal planning and purchasing workflows. This approach reduces cognitive load and delivers a concierge-like experience, with future extensibility to multi-modal inputs such as voice and image recognition.

\textbf{Privacy and Security Measures:}
The system implements strict security mechanisms to protect sensitive user information, including dietary preferences and payment data. It also addresses Large Language Model–specific threats such as prompt injection and jailbreaking through verification-based and zero-trust execution principles.

\textbf{Performance Monitoring and Analytics:}
This component incorporates monitoring and analytics tools to track system responsiveness, inference latency, operational cost, and recommendation accuracy. Key performance indicators include Time to First Token (TTFT) and overall request round-trip time, ensuring both usability and economic feasibility.


The project focuses specifically on the technical and architectural feasibility of such an agent, simulating its deployment within a modern cloud infrastructure using state-of-the-art AI tools like Groq and MongoDB Atlas.

The performance and efficiency of PantryPal’s search and recommendation mechanisms are grounded in algorithmic complexity analysis and optimization techniques covered in this course. The implementation of product search within vector spaces directly applies these algorithmic principles.

R. Sharma and A. Gupta developed 'SmartPantry Manager', a system designed to mitigate household food spoilage through intelligent inventory tracking. Their approach prioritized a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) consumption logic, which dynamically reordered recipes based on the expiration dates of available ingredients. The system demonstrated that prioritizing aging inventory could reduce household food waste by approximately 20\%, validating the necessity of dynamic expiry tracking in modern smart kitchen applications \cite{sharma2024}.



J. Li and Y. Chen explored the intersection of artificial intelligence and behavioral psychology in waste management. Their research highlighted the efficacy of "behavioral nudges," specifically the visualization of financial loss associated with discarded food, to motivate consumer behavior change. They found that users who were presented with a dashboard showing the monetary value of wasted items were 15\% more likely to adopt sustainable consumption habits compared to those receiving generic alerts \cite{li2024}.



M. Rahman and T. Islam focused on the technical architecture of waste reduction systems, implementing a full-stack solution using the MERN (MongoDB, Express, React, Node.js) stack. Their work on the 'Smart Food Waste Management System' demonstrated the scalability and real-time performance of JavaScript-based architectures for handling concurrent inventory updates. This study provides a strong architectural precedent for using non-relational databases to manage the high-velocity data inherent in grocery tracking \cite{rahman2025}.



S. Kumar and V. Singh addressed the "cold start" problem in meal planning by developing a recipe recommendation engine based on ingredient availability. Utilizing Jaccard similarity coefficients, their algorithm matched user inventory lists against a recipe database to suggest meals that maximized current stock usage. While effective for strict keyword matching, their findings suggested that semantic understanding was necessary to handle ingredient substitutions effectively \cite{kumar2024}.



L. Van der Meer and D. Kline expanded the scope of grocery recommendation systems beyond user preference to include environmental sustainability. They introduced a multi-objective optimization algorithm that balanced user taste profiles with the carbon footprint of products. Their results indicated that users were willing to accept "greener" substitutes if the system provided transparent reasoning, a concept that aligns with the growing demand for ethical AI in retail \cite{vandermeer2024}.



J. Park and H. Kim investigated hardware-centric solutions for inventory management, proposing a smart refrigerator equipped with internal cameras and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Their system achieved high accuracy in object detection for common fruits and vegetables. However, they noted significant barriers to adoption due to high hardware costs and privacy concerns, suggesting that software-based inference models might offer a more accessible alternative for mass-market adoption \cite{park2021}.



M. Mancini and L. Rossi integrated IoT technology into the kitchen environment, utilizing connected scales to automate the tracking of food consumption. By linking physical weight data with a mobile application, they were able to update inventory levels in real-time without manual user input. Their study emphasized that minimizing manual data entry is the single most critical factor in sustaining long-term user engagement with food management apps \cite{mancini2023}.



N. Al-Fayed developed a predictive grocery ordering system that analyzed historical purchase data to forecast household needs. By calculating individual consumption intervals for staples like milk and eggs, the system could autonomously populate shopping carts. The study showed that predictive replenishment significantly reduced the cognitive load on consumers, transforming grocery shopping from a proactive chore into a managed service \cite{alfayed2025}.



W. Zhang and Q. Liu applied unsupervised machine learning techniques to personalize the shopping experience. Using K-Means clustering, they grouped users based on purchasing behaviors to recommend products popular within similar demographics. While this method improved recommendation relevance by 18\%, the authors acknowledged the limitations of static clustering in capturing immediate, context-specific user intents compared to more dynamic conversational models \cite{zhang2022}.



B. Davis and E. Thompson examined the impact of Conversational AI on the digital retail experience. Their research indicated that natural language interfaces could handle up to 80\% of routine shopping interactions, such as searching for products or checking order status. They concluded that replacing complex graphical user interfaces (GUIs) with chat-based agents significantly reduced the "decision fatigue" associated with large e-commerce catalogs \cite{davis2024}.




\section{Related Works}

The digital grocery ecosystem can be analyzed through cognitive psychology, consumer behavior, and technological capability. Cognitive Load Theory explains how excessive interface complexity overwhelms users’ limited working memory, particularly in grocery shopping, which involves frequent, high-volume, and multi-item decision-making. Existing platforms amplify extraneous cognitive load through deep category hierarchies and prolonged checkout processes, leading to high cart abandonment. PantryPal mitigates this friction by transferring navigation and decision-making responsibilities from the user to an intelligent agent, thereby reducing unnecessary cognitive effort.

The evolution from Graphical User Interfaces to Conversational User Interfaces marks a shift from system-driven interaction to intent-driven communication. Unlike GUIs, which require users to adapt to rigid structures, conversational interfaces allow natural language expression of needs. Early voice commerce systems were constrained by limited language understanding, but recent advances in Large Language Models enable contextual reasoning and nuanced intent interpretation. Conversational shopping has been shown to improve accessibility and align well with habitual grocery purchasing patterns, fostering repeat behavior and brand loyalty.

Food waste remains a critical inefficiency across both retail and household levels, largely due to the disconnect between purchasing and consumption. Consumers often lack tools for inventory awareness and meal planning, leading to spoilage. Intelligent nudging, such as recipe suggestions based on existing pantry items, and subscription-based purchasing models help reduce waste by improving consumption planning and demand predictability.

Industry trends demonstrate growing validation of AI-driven grocery experiences, including conversational search, mission-based shopping, and sustainability-focused retail models. PantryPal integrates these fragmented approaches into a unified system, positioning a persistent AI agent as an operating system for the kitchen that manages discovery, planning, purchasing, and consumption holistically.

This phase involved the Project Manager and a Domain Expert in grocery retail to conduct requirement gathering and problem scoping. \subsubsection{Testing and Quality Assurance Phase}
Quality Assurance engineers and Security Analysts were responsible for validating system correctness, robustness, and security. This phase included unit testing, integration testing, and adversarial testing such as red-teaming exercises to identify vulnerabilities like prompt injection. Automated testing frameworks and security scanning tools were extensively used to ensure reliability and safety.

\subsubsection{Deployment and Maintenance Phase}
Deployment was managed by DevOps engineers who provisioned and maintained the production environment using cloud infrastructure. Post-deployment, resources were allocated for continuous monitoring and observability through performance dashboards that tracked inference latency, error rates, and system health to ensure long-term stability and scalability.

The system was designed to analyze user intent (symptoms or hunger), provide primary assessments (dietary needs), and recommend appropriate solutions (products ). \textbf{Scalability:} The architecture must support thousands of concurrent users, facilitated by the stateless nature of the LLM and the horizontal scalability of MongoDB Atlas.

\textbf{Security:} The system must implement robust input sanitization to prevent prompt injection attacks and protect user privacy.

\section{System Design and Implementation}

\subsection{Architectural Overview}
The system utilizes a \textbf{Client-Server Architecture} based on the \textbf{MERN Stack} (MongoDB, Express.js, React/Next.js, Node.js). The application is decoupled into two primary layers. The \textbf{Frontend (Client)} is built with Next.js, serving as the user interface for inventory visualization, order management, and AI interaction; it communicates with the server via RESTful APIs. The \textbf{Backend (Server)} is built with Node.js and Express.js, handling business logic, authentication, and database operations; it integrates with external AI services, specifically the Groq SDK, for advanced chatbot capabilities. This separation of concerns ensures modularity, simplifies maintenance, and facilitates independent scaling of the user interface and server-side components.

\subsection{Microservices-Based AI Agent Architecture}
To support the conversational AI shopping assistant, the system implements a dedicated \textbf{Microservices Architecture} that operates in parallel to the core MERN application. This design ensures modularity, scalability, and robust execution. The \textbf{Orchestrator Service}, implemented in Python/Flask, acts as the central brain of the agent. It is responsible for managing conversational state, routing user requests, validating all inputs to prevent injection attacks, and coordinating the workflow between all other specialized services. The \textbf{Inference Service} serves as the dedicated interface to Groq's Language Processing Units (LPUs) for running the Llama 3 model. This service handles the intricate construction of context-aware system prompts and the subsequent parsing of the LLM's structured JSON output into actionable commands. For long-term memory and semantic understanding, the \textbf{Memory Service} leverages MongoDB Atlas Vector Search. It stores high-dimensional embeddings of product descriptions and user preference profiles, enabling the semantic product search functionality that allows users to find items using conceptual queries like "spicy sauce" instead of exact keywords. Finally, a critical security and reliability component is the \textbf{Tool Executor}. This is a deterministic code module that performs safe, validated API calls to the core system (e.g., \texttt{add_to_cart}, \texttt{get_inventory}). By isolating these actions from the non-deterministic LLM, it ensures the agent's actions are always valid, safe, and never result in the addition of non-existent or hallucinated products to the user's cart.

\subsection{Database Design}
The data persistence layer is implemented using \textbf{MongoDB}, a flexible NoSQL document store. To maintain high data integrity and minimize redundancy—a critical consideration for transactional and inventory systems—a \textbf{Relational Reference Strategy} is employed using Mongoose \texttt{ObjectId} references between collections. This hybrid approach combines the scalability of NoSQL with the data consistency benefits of relational models.

\subsubsection{Schema Definitions}
The primary data models are designed for efficiency and clarity. The \textbf{User Schema} stores authentication credentials, including securely hashed passwords, and essential user profile data. The \textbf{Product Schema} acts as the immutable ``Source of Truth'' for all static item details, containing fields such as \texttt{name}, \texttt{price}, \texttt{imageUrl}, and \texttt{shelfLifeDays}. A key design choice is evident in the \textbf{PantryItem Schema}, which represents items currently owned by a user. Instead of duplicating static data like \texttt{imageUrl} or \texttt{price}, this schema stores a reference to the corresponding \texttt{Product} document (\texttt{ref: 'Product'}). It includes dynamic, user-specific fields like \texttt{expiryDate}, \texttt{purchaseDate}, and \texttt{status}. This normalization prevents data anomalies. The \textbf{Order Schema} records the complete transaction history, while the \textbf{Cart Schema} provides temporary storage for items prior to checkout, enabling features like the AI agent's cart management.

\subsection{System Logic Flow}
\subsubsection{Order-to-Pantry Synchronization}
The core application logic automates inventory management upon purchase completion. This process is triggered when a user initiates a \texttt{POST /api/orders} request. The system first performs a lookup to retrieve the static \texttt{shelfLifeDays} value from the referenced document in the Product collection. It then calculates the expiry date using the formula $Expiry = Today + ShelfLifeDays$. Finally, for each item in the order, a new document is created and persisted in the \texttt{PantryItems} collection, linked to the specific user. This automated flow ensures the user's digital pantry is always accurately synchronized with their purchase history, forming the foundation for features like expiry tracking and waste reduction recipes.

\subsection{Implementation of Core Features}
\subsubsection{AI-Powered Cart Management and Semantic Search}
The conversational agent is implemented as a context-aware system that goes beyond simple text generation. To enable reliable cart management, the system employs \textbf{Context Injection}. Before each query is sent to the LLM, the user's current cart contents and a list of available store inventory are dynamically injected into a structured system prompt. The AI is then instructed to return its decisions in a strict \textbf{Structured Output} format, specifically JSON (e.g., \texttt{{"action": "ADD_TO_CART", "productName": "Organic Milk"}}). Backend parsing logic extracts this JSON, validates the intended action and product, and passes the command to the deterministic Tool Executor for safe execution. For semantic search, user queries are converted into vector embeddings via the LLM and compared against pre-stored product embeddings in the MongoDB Atlas Vector Search index, allowing for retrieval based on conceptual similarity rather than keyword matching.

\subsubsection{Dynamic Frontend Data Presentation}
The frontend, built with Next.js, leverages server-side rendering for initial data loading to enhance performance and SEO, particularly for the user dashboard. To display pantry items with their associated product details (images, prices), the backend uses Mongoose's \texttt{populate} method. This performs a runtime "join," fetching the linked \texttt{Product} document data when retrieving \texttt{PantryItem} records, thereby efficiently reuniting normalized data for the UI. Furthermore, the dashboard implements an \textbf{Expiry Visualization Algorithm}. A utility function calculates the days remaining for each item and determines a visual cue (e.g., color coding from green to red) based on the percentage of shelf life consumed, providing users with an immediate, intuitive understanding of their inventory's status.

\subsection{Technology Stack Synthesis}
The implementation synthesizes a modern, full-stack technology suite. The \textbf{Frontend} utilizes Next.js 14 with the App Router, Tailwind CSS for styling, and the React Context API for state management. The \textbf{Core Backend} is built on Node.js and Express.js, with the Mongoose ODM for database interaction. The \textbf{Database} is hosted on MongoDB Atlas, providing global scalability and integrated Vector Search capabilities. The \textbf{AI/Agent Layer} utilizes the Groq SDK for high-speed inference on models like Llama 3 70B, facilitated by Groq's LPUs, and a Python/Flask microservice for agent orchestration. This stack is chosen for its developer efficiency, performance, and ability to support both real-time user interactions and complex AI-driven workflows.

\subsection{Comprehensive Testing Strategy}
A rigorous, multi-layered testing strategy was implemented to ensure the reliability, security, and performance of the Smart Pantry system. This approach spanned from isolated unit tests to holistic user experience evaluations, guaranteeing that each functional component operated correctly both independently and within the integrated ecosystem.

\subsection{Unit Testing of Isolated Components}
Unit testing formed the foundation of the quality assurance process, targeting discrete modules in isolation. For the backend, this involved validating the core business logic within controllers. The Mongoose data models (\texttt{User}, \texttt{Product}, \texttt{PantryItem}) were also unit-tested to enforce schema validation, ensuring required fields like \texttt{price} and \texttt{imageUrl} were present and that default values, such as \texttt{status: 'Fresh'}, were applied upon document creation. On the frontend, individual React components including the \texttt{StatCard} for dashboard metrics and the \texttt{ExpiryAlert} for visual warnings were tested to verify they rendered correctly with various data props and did not crash under edge-case conditions like null or undefined values.

\subsection{Integration Testing for System Cohesion}
Integration testing verified the correct interaction and data flow between interconnected system modules. A critical test suite focused on the end-to-end \textbf{Order-to-Pantry Synchronization Flow}. This involved simulating a complete checkout process through the API to confirm that the creation of a new \texttt{Order} document successfully triggered the automatic generation of corresponding \texttt{PantryItem} documents in the database. Tests validated that the referential integrity was maintained, with each \texttt{PantryItem} correctly linked to its source \texttt{Product} via the stored \texttt{ObjectId}. Another major integration test involved the \textbf{AI Agent's Interaction with the Database}. Natural language commands (e.g., "Add two bananas to my cart" or "Clear my cart") were sent to the AI chat endpoint. The tests then verified that the parsed JSON output from the LLM correctly translated into the appropriate, deterministic API calls, resulting in the expected modifications to the user's \texttt{Cart} collection in MongoDB, thereby ensuring the agent could reliably perform its core task.

\subsection{Functional and Acceptance Testing}
Functional testing was conducted to ensure the system met all specified business requirements and user stories. The \textbf{Authentication and Authorization} workflow was rigorously tested to confirm that unauthenticated users were blocked from accessing protected routes like \texttt{/api/pantry} and properly redirected to the login page. The logic for \textbf{Visual Expiry Indicators} on the dashboard was validated; items with fewer than three days until expiry were confirmed to trigger the red "Action Needed" alert, ensuring the UI provided accurate and timely warnings. Tests also confirmed the robustness of the \textbf{Data Population and Fallback Logic} in the pantry view, verifying that if a product image URL was missing or failed to load, the system correctly displayed a predefined fallback image, maintaining a consistent user experience even with incomplete data.

\subsection{Performance and Load Testing}
Performance testing was essential to guarantee a responsive user experience under realistic conditions. The \textbf{API Endpoint Latency} of critical paths, particularly the \texttt{getPantry} endpoint, was measured and optimized. Special attention was paid to the overhead introduced by the Mongoose \texttt{.populate('product')} method, which performs a join-like operation. Through query optimization and indexing, response times were maintained under a 200-millisecond threshold, even for users with large inventories. On the frontend, \textbf{Rendering Efficiency} was evaluated by stress-testing the dashboard with pantries containing over 50 items. This ensured that React's rendering cycle and virtual DOM diffing algorithms handled large lists efficiently without noticeable lag or interface jank, providing a smooth scrolling and interaction experience.

\subsection{Security and Data Integrity Testing}
Security testing was paramount given the system handles personal user data and inventory information. \textbf{Data Isolation} tests were performed to verify that a logged-in user could only access and modify their own pantry items, orders, and cart data; attempts to access another user's resources by manipulating user IDs in API requests were confirmed to fail with appropriate authorization errors. \textbf{Input Validation and Sanitization} tests were conducted against all API endpoints to guard against injection attacks. This included sending malformed data types (e.g., strings where numbers were expected) and testing for SQL/NoSQL injection vectors to ensure the system rejected invalid input and maintained data integrity.

\subsection{Usability and Interface Testing}
Usability testing focused on the quality of the human-computer interaction. The \textbf{Modal Interaction} for the Pantry Detail view was tested to ensure it opened and closed smoothly, displayed the correct, dynamically calculated "Freshness Level" progress bar (e.g., showing 93% for a recently added item), and did not disrupt the main application flow. \textbf{Responsive Design} validation was conducted across multiple device profiles and screen sizes. Using Tailwind CSS breakpoints, tests confirmed that the pantry's grid layout, navigation elements, and typography adapted correctly and remained fully functional on mobile phones, tablets, and desktop computers, ensuring accessibility for all users.

The system fundamentally alters the relationship between retailer and consumer, fostering a deeper, more continuous connection. By evolving from a purely transactional model to a managed service paradigm—through personalized subscriptions, proactive replenishment, and contextual meal planning—the retailer becomes an integrated partner in the user's daily household management. This shift increases transparency and builds substantial trust, provided that data privacy and user autonomy are rigorously maintained and communicated as core values.

\subsection{Community Impact}
For users with accessibility challenges, such as visual impairments or motor difficulties, as well as those with lower digital literacy, the Voice-First interface capability of PantryPal plays a critical democratizing role. It significantly lowers the barrier to online grocery shopping by removing dependence on complex visual interfaces with small buttons and deep navigation menus. This makes the convenience and safety of home delivery accessible to a broader demographic, including the elderly and individuals for whom traditional e-commerce platforms are challenging to use.

\subsection{Monitoring and Reporting}
The system incorporates dedicated modules for tracking and visualizing user-centric metrics such as "Food Waste Saved" and "Money Saved." By providing users with clear, positive reinforcement for sustainable behaviors, this feature introduces an element of gamification into household management. This feedback mechanism is designed to drive long-term behavioral change, encouraging more mindful purchasing habits and promoting the consumption of existing inventory before it spoils.

\subsection{Predictive Analytics for Environmental Management}
The aggregation of anonymized consumption data across the user base generates a valuable macro-level dataset. This data can provide policymakers, urban planners, and supply chain managers with unprecedented insights into regional food consumption patterns, peak demand periods, and waste generation. Such analytics can enable more efficient resource distribution, help identify and address food deserts, and inform smarter, data-driven waste management and public health strategies at a municipal or regional level.

\subsection{Sustainable Innovation}
PantryPal embodies a model of sustainable innovation by strategically aligning economic incentives, such as increased sales and customer retention, with critical environmental goals like waste reduction. It demonstrates that profitability and ecological responsibility are not mutually exclusive but can be synergistically coupled through intelligent, AI-driven inventory management. By proactively managing the final stage of the food supply chain—the consumer's home—the system aims to close the loop on household food waste, creating a more resilient and efficient circular economy.



% Chapter 8: Lesson Learned
\chapter{Lesson Learned}

\section{Technical Challenges and Solutions}

\subsection{Problems Faced During Development}
The implementation of the Smart Pantry system presented a series of intricate technical challenges that tested the initial architectural assumptions. These hurdles primarily emerged in two critical domains: ensuring robust data consistency and integrity across the full MERN stack, and achieving reliable, deterministic integration with a generative Large Language Model (LLM). Successfully navigating these issues was fundamental to transforming the conceptual design into a stable, production-ready application.

\subsubsection{Data Redundancy and Schema Inconsistency}
An early and significant design flaw was identified in the initial approach to the \texttt{PantryItem} schema. The schema was designed to denormalize and directly store volatile product attributes such as \texttt{imageUrl} and \texttt{price}. This design led to immediate data synchronization problems. For instance, when a product's price was updated in the central \texttt{Product} catalog for a store-wide sale, the change was not reflected in the thousands of existing \texttt{PantryItem} documents referencing that product, causing users to see outdated information. A related issue surfaced during testing with inconsistently seeded data, resulting in "ghost items" within the user interface—product cards that displayed generic placeholders because the embedded image URLs in the pantry items were broken or missing, severely degrading the user experience and trust in the system's accuracy.

\subsubsection{Date Calculation and Timezone Discrepancies}
A critical logic error manifesting as a poor user experience was discovered in the expiry date calculation. Items added with a defined shelf life (e.g., 14 days) were frequently shown as having "13 days left" immediately after purchase. The root cause was a mismatch in temporal handling between the server and client environments. The backend, operating in UTC, would calculate an expiry date, while the frontend, running in the user's local timezone (e.g., Bangladesh Standard Time, UTC+6), would interpret these dates with an offset. This led to a situation where, due to the timezone difference, an item purchased late in the day locally could be considered by the client-side calculation to have already lost a full calendar day, confusing users and undermining the precision promised by the "smart" tracking feature.

\subsubsection{Unstructured Output from the AI Chatbot}
Integrating the Groq AI SDK introduced a core challenge of non-determinism. While the LLM was powerful at understanding natural language queries like "add milk to my cart," its responses were inherently conversational. It would often return friendly confirmations such as "Sure, I've added two cartons of milk to your shopping cart!" instead of the pure, machine-readable JSON structure required to trigger an automated database update. This unstructured text caused the backend's JSON parsing logic to fail with syntax errors, rendering the AI agent incapable of executing its primary function—autonomously modifying the user's cart. The system was trapped in a dialogue loop without the ability to act.

\subsubsection{Complex State Management for In-Page Modals}
A user experience friction point arose from the initial implementation of the order history review. Clicking on a past order would trigger a full page navigation to a dedicated detail route, which disrupted the user's context, hid the main dashboard, and felt cumbersome. The attempt to refactor this into a modal overlay presented a classic React state management challenge. Passing the selected order's data down through multiple component layers (prop drilling) from the Dashboard to a deeply nested Modal component made the code brittle and difficult to maintain. Furthermore, managing the modal's open/closed state at a high level risked causing unnecessary re-renders of the entire dashboard, potentially impacting performance.

\subsection{Solutions Implemented}
Each identified challenge was met with a targeted solution that not only resolved the immediate issue but also improved the overall system architecture, robustness, and maintainability.

\subsubsection{Adoption of a Normalized Relational Reference Strategy}
To permanently resolve data inconsistency, the database schema was fundamentally refactored. The denormalized \texttt{PantryItem} schema was abandoned in favor of a \textbf{Normalized Reference Model}. In the new design, the \texttt{PantryItem} document no longer stores copies of product details; instead, it holds only a lightweight reference—a MongoDB \texttt{ObjectId}—pointing to the corresponding document in the authoritative \texttt{Product} collection. This structural change was paired with a dynamic data-fetching strategy. In the \texttt{pantryController}, the Mongoose \texttt{.populate('product')} method was implemented. This instructs the database to automatically perform a "join" at query time, fetching the latest \texttt{imageUrl}, \texttt{price}, and \texttt{name} from the \texttt{Product} collection for each \texttt{PantryItem}. This ensures users always see current data. To eliminate "ghost items," a comprehensive database seeding script (\texttt{seedProducts.js}) was developed to pre-populate the product catalog with validated, high-quality image URLs and consistent pricing before any user interaction, guaranteeing a reliable baseline.

\subsubsection{Client-Side Date Normalization with a Utility Function}
The date calculation error was addressed by shifting and standardizing the logic. Instead of performing fragile date arithmetic that compared server-generated UTC timestamps with the client's local time, a dedicated \texttt{getDaysLeft()} utility function was implemented on the frontend. This function takes the \texttt{purchaseDate} and \texttt{shelfLifeDays} as inputs. Its key innovation is to normalize both the purchase date and the current date to their respective "midnight" values in the user's local timezone before calculating the difference. This abstraction effectively removes the confounding factor of time-of-day from the day-count logic, ensuring that an item purchased at 11:59 PM is correctly shown as having a full day remaining, thereby delivering a consistent and intuitive user experience.

\subsubsection{Strict System Prompt Engineering and Robust Output Parsing}
The non-determinism of the AI was constrained through a combination of precise instruction and robust parsing. The System Prompt provided to the Groq LLM was meticulously engineered to mandate a specific output format. It explicitly instructed the model to return \textit{only} a JSON array containing action objects, suppressing any conversational text. To defensively handle any residual non-compliance, a two-stage parsing strategy was deployed on the backend. First, a Regular Expression (Regex) is used to scan the raw AI response for any substring bracketed by \texttt{[} and \texttt{]}. This extracted string, which contains the JSON array, is then passed to \texttt{JSON.parse()}. This approach effectively filters out any unpredictable narrative text, ensuring the backend receives a clean, executable command to pass to the deterministic Tool Executor service, thereby closing the loop between user intent and system action.

\subsubsection{Centralized State Management via React Context for UI Components}
The modal state management problem was solved by implementing a more scalable state solution. The cumbersome prop drilling was eliminated by leveraging React's Context API. A dedicated context (e.g., \texttt{ModalContext}) was created to manage the state of the currently selected order and the modal's visibility. The Dashboard component provides this context, and the Modal component consumes it. This allows the modal to access the necessary data and control its own display state without requiring props to be passed down through every intermediate component. This architectural change resulted in cleaner, more maintainable code, prevented unnecessary re-renders of parent components, and provided a seamless, in-context user experience where details could be viewed and dismissed without losing place on the main dashboard.




% Chapter 9: Future Work & Conclusion
\chapter{Future Work \& Conclusion}

\section{Future Works}

Future iterations of PantryPal will focus on expanding its sensory capabilities and ecosystem integration to become a true "ambient intelligence" in the kitchen.
\textbf{Visual Pantry Audit}
Integrating Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to allow users to upload photos of their fridge or pantry. The agent will identify existing items and automatically populate the inventory, solving the "cold start" data problem and eliminating manual entry.

\textbf{IoT Integration}
Connecting with smart appliances (e.g., smart fridges, connected ovens) to gather real-time consumption data and automate reordering without user intervention. This would move the system from "predictive" to "reactive" based on actual sensor data.

\textbf{Hyper-Personalized Health}
Integrating with health data platforms (e.g., Apple Health, Fitbit) to recommend recipes that align with specific biometric goals (e.g., reducing cholesterol) dynamically. The agent could act as a nutritionist, adjusting the cart based on the user's activity levels.

\textbf{Voice Assistant Integration}
Deploying PantryPal as a skill on Alexa or Google Home to facilitate "hands-free" cooking assistance and inventory management while the user's hands are full in the kitchen.

\section{Conclusion}

The PantryPal project effectively demonstrates that the convergence of Agentic AI, high-speed inference hardware, and semantic search can fundamentally reshape the online grocery experience. By shifting the cognitive load from the human to the machine, the system addresses the critical economic failures of cart abandonment and the environmental tragedy of food waste. The transition from a "Search-based" paradigm to a "Management-based" agentic paradigm offers a path toward a more efficient, sustainable, and user-centric future for retail. While challenges in security and data integration remain, the architecture defined in this report provides a viable blueprint for the next generation of commerce, proving that intelligent agents can be both economically viable and socially responsible. The project confirms that with the right architecture, AI can move beyond being a passive tool to becoming an active partner in our daily lives.
